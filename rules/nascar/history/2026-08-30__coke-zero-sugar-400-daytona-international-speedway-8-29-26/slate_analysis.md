# NASCAR — Coke Zero Sugar 400, Daytona International Speedway (Saturday night, 8/29/26)

## The short version

This part explains the whole slate in plain words. If you read nothing else, read this.

1. This is a Daytona night race, which means the cars run in one big pack and crashes decide almost everything.
2. Points here come mostly from **passing cars**, not from leading laps — DraftKings pays 1 point for every spot a driver gains from where he starts.
3. Over the last nine Daytona races, **77.7% of the best-possible-lineup spots** went to drivers who started 20th or worse (the DFR starting-position chart).
4. Leading laps almost never pays here — only about **1.5 drivers per race** who led double-digit laps also finished with a top-10 fantasy score (Dustin's Deep Dive).
5. The crowd already knows all of this, so the deep starters are the **most-picked drivers this week** (the chalk), not the sneaky ones.
6. That means the one decision that separates entries is not *whether* to start deep — it is **which** deep-starting driver you buy, and at what pick rate.
7. A sharp keeps the popular strong cars and finds his one different piece among the deep starters nobody is picking, like Daniel Suarez at 9% picked.
8. The biggest trap is paying a high pick rate for a car whose own projected score does not justify it — A.J. Allmendinger is picked by 37% of the field while a nearly identical car next to him is picked by 33%.
9. You do not have to spend all $50,000 — **none of the last 12 best-possible Daytona lineups used the full salary cap**.

## Edges & tensions

These are the things that actually matter tonight. They are observations, not instructions.

1. **The back of the field is the chalk, not the contrarian move** — 8 of the 10 most-picked drivers start 21st or worse (DailyFan ownership vs the Deep Dive's starting grid).
2. **Deep starts really do pay here** — 21st-30th is 33.3% of best-lineup spots and worse-than-30th is 44.4% (the DFR nine-race chart).
3. **11th-20th is the dead zone** — that band is 14.8% of best-lineup spots, the worst return for its size (same DFR chart).
4. **Leading laps is close to worthless tonight**, which overrides this repo's standing "two dominator paths" rule — 1.5 drivers per race (Dustin's Deep Dive).
5. **Salary is not binding** — the last 12 best-possible Daytona lineups cost between $31,200 and $49,400, never the full $50,000 (Dustin's Deep Dive).
6. **Anchor-Equivalence:** A.J. Allmendinger ($5,500, 37% picked, starts 36th) and Noah Gragson ($5,400, 33% picked, starts 37th) are near-identical bets at near-identical prices.
7. **Where your opponents reliably go:** Christopher Bell in 3 of your 3 past comparable contests, Bubba Wallace 3 of 3, plus Larson, Blaney, Ty Gibbs, Todd Gilliland and Ryan Preece each in 2 of 3.
8. **The price shape that keeps costing the losing half:** 11 of 17 past traps were picked by 25%+ of the field, and 12 of 24 were picked higher than their projected score ranked.
9. **Tonight's cars with that same shape** (picked higher than their projection earns): Alex Bowman, Allmendinger, Austin Dillon, Tyler Reddick, Gilliland and Zane Smith.
10. **Duplication watch:** the field pairs Bell + Denny Hamlin in roughly 18.2% of lineups — about **214 of the 1,176 entries** before the race even starts.

## Field vs Sharp — how this slate gets played

**How the FIELD will play it.** The typical entry is six deep starters, because every article this week says to stack the back. Expect a core of Bell (48% picked), Hamlin (38%), Ricky Stenhouse Jr (37%), Allmendinger (37%), Ryan Blaney (36%) and Gragson (33%) — and your past contests show the top three most-picked drivers landing together in 18.7% of entries.

**How a SHARP will play it.** The pros who play your NASCAR contests are chalky: they average **35.0% ownership per roster slot** and hold the popular strong cars, with a truly low-owned piece in only about 1 in 22 lineups. Their real separation is which deep starter they buy, and they keep every roster unique — 97.7% of their entries are one-of-a-kind. The one pro who does it differently is **moklovin** (seen in 15 of your contests, beat you 5 times), who sits lower at 29.8% per slot and runs a low-owned piece in about 1 of every 4 lineups.

**The gap.** You currently run **28.7% ownership per slot** against their 35.0%, and that same gap has been your biggest structural difference in 3 of the last 5 slates. On this slate the divide is simple: the field is paying 26-48% pick rates for deep starters, while the identical 21st-30th start band is available at 9-13% in Suarez, Josh Berry and John H. Nemechek.

## Build it like a sharp

You play one lineup per contest, so each entry is your whole tournament. This walks through the decisions in the order a sharp makes them. These are suggestions about how a sharp thinks — you make every call.

**1. The anchor decision.** A sharp anchors this slate with an expensive car that starts DEEP, because expensive cars starting up front have no room to gain. The candidates are Bell (48% picked, starts 35th), Elliott (29%, 22nd) and Keselowski (32%, 21st), with Bubba Wallace (26%, 26th) as the fourth. The Anchor-Equivalence twins to notice are Hamlin/Stenhouse at 38%/37% and Allmendinger/Gragson at 37%/33%. Important scope note: at pack tracks like this one, several popular strong cars tend to finish **together**, so a sharp does not cap himself at one of them — at Atlanta this year the winning lineup in both of your contests ran three chalk anchors at once.

**2. The leverage piece.** A sharp's different piece comes from the 21st-30th start band, because that band is 33.3% of best-lineup spots and the crowd is buying it at the wrong prices. Named candidates: Suarez (9% picked), Berry (10%), Nemechek (13%) and Cody Ware (1%). The pros in your contests carry a truly low-owned piece in only about 1 of every 22 lineups, so this is a choice, not a requirement — carrying none is fine if you looked at each one and passed.

**3. What a sharp refuses.** A sharp does not carry both Bell and Hamlin: about 214 of the 1,176 entries already have that pair, so the entry starts the night sharing its identity with 214 opponents. He also notices that in your past comparable contests only 70.4% of entries were unique rosters and the single most-copied lineup appeared 85 times — copying the obvious build is the most common mistake in this field, not a safe one.

**4. The salary shape.** The cap is $50,000 across 6 drivers, which averages $8,333 each. Anchoring on Bell ($10,200) leaves $39,800 for five cars, or $7,960 each — still comfortable. Anchoring on Bell **and** Blaney ($10,500) leaves $29,300 for four, or $7,325 each, which forces cheap deep cars into the last slots. But remember the track history: winning Daytona lineups have cost as little as $31,200, so the real question is not "did I spend it all" — it is **which popular car gets dropped** when Bell, Hamlin, Stenhouse and Blaney cannot all fit.

**5. Two contests, two different entries.** The $12K contest has 1,176 entries and the $4K has 392, and both pay out heavily to the top. In the 1,176-entry field a sharp needs a genuinely low-picked piece to be unique, because with that many opponents the popular combinations get copied. In the 392-entry field the math is looser: three drivers picked in the 10-18% range are already enough to make the roster one-of-a-kind, so the differentiation can be gentler and the anchors can stay stronger.

**6. The pre-lock check.** Five yes/no questions, all drawn from tonight's reads:

- Do at least 4 of my 6 drivers start 21st or worse, where 77.7% of best-lineup spots come from?
- Does my lineup hold both Bell and Hamlin, the pair about 214 opponents already have?
- Is anyone in my lineup starting inside the top 7, the group the Deep Dive says to strongly consider avoiding?
- Did I spend all $50,000 out of habit, when the last 12 Daytona best-lineups never did?
- Can I say in one sentence what this lineup needs to happen tonight that the crowd's lineup does not?

**The envelope target:** about 35% ownership per roster slot with a unique roster — or moklovin's shape, 29.8% per slot with one genuinely low-picked piece, which is the pro who has beaten you 5 times in 15 tries.
