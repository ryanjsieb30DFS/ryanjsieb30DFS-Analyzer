## Edges & tensions

This section is the short list of things that actually matter today. Each line is one observation and the number or article line that shows it. These are not instructions — they are the facts you make your own decisions from.

**1. The most-picked golfers do not fit under the salary cap together.** The six highest-owned golfers are Michael Brennan (37.6% owned), Cameron Young (32.6%), Rickie Fowler (27.2%), Xander Schauffele (26.0%), Chris Kirk (25.4%) and Ryan Gerard (25.3%) per the DK projection file. Added up their salaries are $55,800. The cap is $50,000. So no team on earth can hold all six. Every single entry in both contests has to leave at least one of them out. That forced choice is where teams separate from each other.

**2. Michael Brennan is the crowd's center of gravity.** He is 37.6% owned in the DK file — that means about 4 of every 10 teams will have him. The ETR R4 article says his ownership could "come up to the 40s," so the real number may be even higher. He costs $8,600 and starts T-2, which is the cheapest way to own a golfer who might win the tournament.

**3. History says the tournament winner usually IS in the winning DFS team.** The ETR historical chart in this week's article shows 72.2% of best-finishing Showdown lineups contained the actual tournament winner. Right now the two golfers most likely to win the tournament are Davis Riley (he leads) at $6,400 and Michael Brennan (T-2) at $8,600. Both are cheap for that role. The article separately argues Riley has "been atrocious this year" and is a bad play. That is a straight disagreement between the historical structure and the writer's read.

**4. Same price, same projection, very different ownership — Chris Kirk and Matt Wallace.** Both cost exactly $7,600 in the DK file. Kirk projects 34.8 points, Wallace projects 34.5 points — a difference of three tenths of a point, which is nothing. But Kirk is 25.4% owned and Wallace is 16.0% owned. So roughly 1 in 4 teams has Kirk and roughly 1 in 6 has Wallace, for the same money and the same expected score. **This is the Anchor-Equivalence observation** (two golfers the field treats as interchangeable, priced the same, owned very differently). The article agrees, saying Wallace "gets a slight ownership discount to some of these true T-10 players."

**5. A second Anchor-Equivalence pair sits in the $9,000 range — Ryan Gerard and Russell Henley.** Gerard is $9,200 and projects 38.7 points. Henley is $9,100 and projects 37.0 points. That is $100 and 1.7 points apart, which is close to identical. Gerard is 25.3% owned and Henley is 17.1% owned. So Gerard costs you 8 extra points of ownership for 1.7 points of projection. The article lumps them together too: "Gerard (and Russell Henley) are taking on just as much ownership as Xander, despite the price difference."

**6. A third equivalence pair sits at the top — Xander Schauffele and Si Woo Kim.** Xander is $10,400 / 26.0% owned / 39.1 projected. Si Woo is $10,000 / 17.2% owned / 37.3 projected. Same price shelf, same kind of golfer, and Si Woo is owned about 9 points less. The article expects both to actually come in below their projected ownership because "people flock to the cheaper 9Ks to pair with Cam Young."

**7. Paying up buys almost nothing in projected points.** Cameron Young costs $11,000 and projects 40.7 points in the DK file. Rasmus Hojgaard costs $6,300 and projects 32.7 points. That is $4,700 of salary for 8 points of projection. Put another way, Young gives you 3.7 projected points for every $1,000 you spend, while Rasmus gives you 5.2. The whole projection sheet is squeezed into a narrow band — the top 40 golfers are separated by only about 10 points. When projections are that flat, ownership does almost all the work of separating teams.

**8. The crowd will pair Brennan with a big name, over and over.** Using the projected ownership, here is how often the field lands on the same two golfers together in the 7,134-entry contest:

| Pair | Share of the field | Roughly how many teams |
|---|---|---|
| Michael Brennan + Cameron Young | 12.3% | ~877 of 7,134 |
| Michael Brennan + Rickie Fowler | 10.2% | ~728 |
| Michael Brennan + Xander Schauffele | 9.8% | ~699 |
| Michael Brennan + Chris Kirk | 9.6% | ~685 |
| Michael Brennan + Ryan Gerard | 9.5% | ~678 |
| Cameron Young + Rickie Fowler | 8.9% | ~635 |

Reading the top line: a team holding both Brennan and Young starts the day sharing those two picks with roughly 877 other teams. In the 245-entry single-entry contest that same pair is shared with about 30 of your 244 opponents. This is a duplication note (how many other people made the same choice), not a reason to break the pair — that call is yours.

**9. The cheap end is where the crowd is hiding, and the article thinks it is a trap.** Davis Riley ($6,400, 21.7% owned) and Rasmus Hojgaard ($6,300, 19.4% owned) are each on about 1 in 5 teams. The article's reason is money, not golf: they "are going to be quite popular with Cam Young builds and other builds that require true salary relief." The writer's worry is that the field piles in "so the field can stay out of the 8Ks," making them "ridiculously bad plays." Note the tension with point 3 — Riley is also the golfer currently leading the tournament.

**10. The course is playing easier than the venue file expected.** The Detroit Golf Club file in our notes says ETR guessed the rebuilt par-70 layout would play "2–3 strokes harder overall." Three rounds in, the article says "we know there are -7s or better on this course" and "this tournament is quite obviously going to result in scoring for R4." So the pre-tournament guess is being contradicted by actual play. That matters because low scoring lets golfers starting far back climb, which widens who can win.

**11. There is no second opinion this week.** Only one projection source is loaded — DK's own Round 4 Showdown file, 73 golfers. Normally we cross-check two vendors against each other and treat their disagreement as signal. That is not available. So every projection number below comes from one place, and the only second opinion in this document is the ETR article's written read.

## Field vs Sharp — how this slate gets played

This section puts the crowd's likely team next to a sharp player's likely team, so you can see exactly where they split.

### How the FIELD will play it

The typical entry starts with Michael Brennan, because he is 37.6% owned and he is the cheapest golfer near the top of the leaderboard at $8,600. From there the crowd adds one big name — Cameron Young at $11,000 (32.6% owned) is the most common partner, at roughly 877 of 7,134 teams. That pairing costs $19,600 of the $50,000 cap, leaving $30,400 for four golfers, or an average of $7,600 each. That is exactly why the article warns about the cheap end: the money forces those teams down into the $6,000 range, where Davis Riley (21.7%) and Rasmus Hojgaard (19.4%) are waiting.

The crowd will also over-fill the middle. Chris Kirk at 25.4% and Kris Ventura at 22.0% are the two most-owned golfers in the $7,000 range, and the article calls Ventura "probably the shakiest chalk" on the board. Michael Thorbjornsen is 21.9% owned at $8,800 and the article never mentions him at all — that is popularity running on the projection sheet alone.

Add it up and the field's shape is heavy. A common team of Brennan + Young + Fowler + Kirk already carries 122.8% of combined ownership across just four of its six slots. The ETR historical chart says winning Showdown lineups average about 106.6% combined ownership across all six slots, and our own three-year study of 146 real DK slates puts winners at about 115%. So the crowd's default team is already past the winning shape before it fills the last two spots.

### How a SHARP will play it

Our shark data comes from the pros who actually played your golf contests. Their observed shape is 16.1% average ownership per roster slot, a sub-5%-owned golfer in 48.9% of their lineups, and 89.6% of their lineups completely unique. Your own logged numbers are 15.6% ownership per slot, a sub-5% golfer in 39.7% of lineups, and anchor exposure 0.37 against their 0.41. So you are already near the pros on ownership. The gap is the leverage piece (a golfer almost nobody owns) — they carry one about half the time, you carry one about four times in ten.

There is a format wrinkle worth knowing. That 16.1% number blends two-day Classic golf with Showdown. Our own 146-slate study of Round 4 Showdown specifically says winners here run about **19% average ownership per golfer** with about **1.7 golfers under 10% owned** — chalkier than Classic. So a sharp does not go wildly contrarian in this format. They pay for one or two popular golfers and take their difference in one or two spots.

On this slate that points a sharp to the $7,000 range. The article says it directly: "If you like low owned players with upside, however, this is the range for you." Johnny Keefer ($7,700, 7.9% owned), Marco Penge ($7,500, 9.7%), Emiliano Grillo ($7,700, 7.8%), Stephan Jaeger ($7,200, 6.5%) and Sungjae Im ($7,300, 9.1%) all project between 33.0 and 33.6 points — within half a point of Kris Ventura's 33.7 at 22.0% owned. Same expected score, one third of the ownership.

### The gap

The field pays about 25% ownership for Chris Kirk at $7,600 and gets 34.8 projected points. A sharp pays about 16% ownership for Matt Wallace at the exact same $7,600 and gets 34.5 projected points — or drops to 8% ownership for Ricky Castillo at $7,100 and gets 33.1. That is the whole slate in one sentence: the $7,000 range has eight or nine golfers projecting within two points of each other, and the crowd has crammed itself onto two of them.

### How a sharp USES the key players

This is guidance on how the sharp archetype holds each golfer, with the ownership and salary math. It is not a team and not a set of picks — you choose the golfers.

**Michael Brennan ($8,600, 37.6% owned, projects 38.0).** The field is near 40% and the article thinks it goes higher. A sharp does not fade him to zero — our ledger has a codified rule that zeroing a top anchor is a voluntary exit from the contest, and it cost us the RBC Canadian Open slate when Matt Fitzpatrick was taken to 0% and finished the second-highest scorer. But a sharp holds him knowing what it costs. If you carry Brennan at 37.6%, the other five slots have to average about 15.5% ownership to land near the 115% winning shape. Add Cameron Young at 32.6% and the remaining four have to average about 11%. Add Rickie Fowler on top of those two and the last three slots must average under 6% each. What a sharp refuses around him is the second and third piece of popular chalk — Brennan plus Young plus Fowler is a team that has already spent its whole ownership budget in three slots.

**Cameron Young ($11,000, 32.6% owned, projects 40.7).** He is the highest-projected golfer and the most expensive. The article's verdict is lukewarm: "Young is ok but expensive." The construction cost is the real story. Anchoring Young leaves $39,000 for five golfers, an average of $7,800 each, which is workable. Anchoring Young *and* Brennan leaves $30,400 for four, an average of $7,600 — still workable. Anchoring Young, Brennan and any $9,000+ golfer drops the last three slots to roughly $6,300 each, which is precisely the Riley/Rasmus tier the article calls a trap. A sharp who uses Young usually skips one of the other big names to keep the bottom of the team out of the $6,000 bargain bin.

**Xander Schauffele ($10,400, 26.0% owned) and Si Woo Kim ($10,000, 17.2% owned).** The article expects both to finish under their projected ownership, and names both among "the 4 guys I'll be focused on." They are the same bet at a $400 price difference. A sharp who wants this tier takes the cheaper, less-owned side unless something specific separates them, because 9 points of ownership is a real edge and 1.8 points of projection is not.

**Ryan Gerard ($9,200, 25.3% owned) and Russell Henley ($9,100, 17.1% owned).** Gerard's ownership is climbing because his projection climbed with his leaderboard position — the article notes DK "aggressively priced some players as they rise on the leaderboard" and that Gerard is "taking on the second most ownership due to his really strong projection." That is ownership generated by a number, not by a separate read. The article's own reaction is "Gerard/Henley feel weird to me." A sharp treats these two as one bet and takes it at the lower ownership, or skips the tier entirely.

**Chris Kirk ($7,600, 25.4% owned).** The article is going the other way here — "Chris Kirk is someone the field has not believed in for a long time... I might be overweight on Kirk and underweight on Ventura." So the writer is deliberately buying the more popular of that pair. That is a real disagreement with the leverage math, and it is worth knowing the article's stated cap: "max 1 of Chris Kirk and Ventura." Carrying both means paying 47.4 points of combined ownership for two golfers in the same price tier.

**Davis Riley ($6,400, 21.7% owned) and Rasmus Hojgaard ($6,300, 19.4% owned).** These two are near-identical: $100 apart, 32.2 vs 32.7 projected, both around 20% owned. A sharp who needs the salary relief takes one, not both, because holding both is one bet paid for twice and it puts 41% of combined ownership into your two cheapest slots. The article's stance is a "sprinkle of Rasmus... minimal."

**The Brennan + Young combo (~877 teams of 7,134).** A sharp either skips one side of it, or accepts it only if the other four slots are genuinely unusual. Carrying both halves and then filling with Fowler, Kirk and Riley produces a team that is barely distinguishable from several hundred others, which is the exact failure our 5.31.26 autopsy recorded as clustering just under the cash line.

## Leverage

This section names the golfers almost nobody is picking who could still decide the slate. "Leverage" just means low ownership plus real upside — if he goes off, very few opponents have him. The rule here is that **every price range gets screened**, including ranges the article never discussed, because the golfer who wins a Showdown from nowhere is usually one the articles skipped. No fade calls live in this section.

**Why this screen is the priority this week:** our last logged Round 4 Showdown (RBC Canadian Open, 6/19) scored **0% leverage capture** — none of the low-owned golfers who decided the slate were on any of our six entries. That is a process signal, and it makes this screen the most important part of the document.

There is no leverage here, and that is worth saying out loud. Cameron Young is 32.6% owned, Xander Schauffele 26.0%, and Si Woo Kim 17.2%. ## Fades

This section names popular golfers whose ownership looks expensive for what they have to do. A fade is a bet, not a fact — you are betting the crowd is wrong. Three strengths are used below. **FADE** means the case against is strongest. **LEAN FADE** means the case against is real but not overwhelming. **UNDERWEIGHT** means you would own him, just less than the field does.

- **Davis Riley** ($6,400, 21.7% owned) — **LEAN FADE.** The article's case: he has "been atrocious this year," the ownership is coming from teams needing "true salary relief," and "there's a reason why he was likely less than 1% owned in R1 showdown as recently as this week." The world this fade needs is the one the article expects — the leader folds and the 1 in 5 teams holding him lose a slot. The reason this is a lean and not a full fade: he currently leads the tournament, and 72.2% of best-finishing Showdown lineups historically contain the eventual tournament winner.
- **Kris Ventura** ($7,100, 22.0% owned) — **LEAN FADE.** The article calls him "probably the shakiest chalk" and plans to be "underweight on Ventura to make sure I'm not taking on too much ownership in this range." The world this needs is the one where the six sub-10%-owned golfers in his own price range — Penge, Keefer, Grillo, Sungjae, Castillo, Jaeger, all projecting within 0.6 points of him — outscore him. That is a coin flip he is 13 ownership points more expensive than.
- **Rasmus Hojgaard** ($6,300, 19.4% owned) — **UNDERWEIGHT.** Same salary-relief story as Riley, and the article's own plan is "a sprinkle of Rasmus... minimal." He does have the best projected value per dollar on the board, which is why this is underweight and not a fade.
- **Ryan Gerard** ($9,200, 25.3% owned) — **UNDERWEIGHT.** His projection is real — 38.7 points, fourth-best on the board. But 8 of those ownership points are being paid for 1.7 projected points over Russell Henley at $9,100. The world this needs is the one where the $9,000 tier's two near-identical golfers split roughly evenly, which is what our Anchor-Equivalence rule has been recording for four straight slates.
- **Michael Thorbjornsen** ($8,800, 21.9% owned) — **UNDERWEIGHT.** He is chalk with no written case anywhere in the article. About 1 in 5 teams will own a golfer whose entire argument is a projection number, on a slate where the top 40 projections are separated by only 10 points.
- **The Brennan + Cameron Young pair together** — **UNDERWEIGHT as a pair, not as golfers.** Roughly 877 of 7,134 teams and about 30 of your 244 single-entry opponents will hold both. Neither golfer is a bad play. The observation is only that holding both spends 70.2 ownership points in two slots, which forces the remaining four to average about 11% each to reach the winning shape.

## Field attack plan

This section is only for the 150-entry contest (the $3K mini-MAX, field 7,134, top-heavy payouts, 34 entries). In a big field you do not win by projecting better than everyone else. You win by standing on the other side of the mistakes the crowd makes over and over. Below are the crowd's recurring mistakes that have real evidence on THIS slate, each with one sentence naming the mistake and one naming the attack.

**1. The field copies itself.** The most likely duplicated builds this week all start with Michael Brennan — Brennan + Young at roughly 877 of 7,134 teams, Brennan + Fowler at ~728, Brennan + Xander at ~699, Brennan + Kirk at ~685, Brennan + Gerard at ~678. The attack is to know which of those pairs your entry carries and to make sure the other four slots are not also the crowd's four, because a team that shares its pair AND its filler is not really a separate entry.

**2. Ownership is mispriced where the public numbers are wrong.** Two golfers at the exact same $7,600 price and within three tenths of a point in projection carry 25.4% and 16.0% ownership (Kirk and Wallace); two golfers $100 apart in price and 1.7 points apart in projection carry 25.3% and 17.1% (Gerard and Henley). The attack is to buy the identical bet on the cheaper ownership side across a meaningful share of your 34 entries, because the pricing error repeats in every one of them.

**3. The field chases what just happened.** Ryan Gerard's ownership climbed with his leaderboard position — the article says DK "aggressively priced some players as they rise on the leaderboard" and that Gerard is "taking on the second most ownership due to his really strong projection." The attack is to notice that his ownership and his price both moved for the same reason, so you are paying twice for one Saturday.

**4. The field herds onto one of two equal anchors.** This slate has three of these matched pairs: Xander (26.0%) versus Si Woo Kim (17.2%), Gerard (25.3%) versus Henley (17.1%), and Kirk (25.4%) versus Wallace (16.0%). In a 34-entry set the underowned twin is not a one-lineup pivot — it is a sized position, and our ledger has this exact leak recorded on four consecutive golf slates including the CJ Cup, where 100% exposure to one chalk anchor and 0% to its twin cost the slate when the twin scored 97.8.

**5. The field skips the mid-price scorer tier.** Six golfers between $7,100 and $7,700 — Penge, Keefer, Grillo, Sungjae, Castillo and Jaeger — all project 33.0 to 33.6 points and all sit under 10% owned, while Kris Ventura at $7,100 projects 33.7 at 22.0% owned. The attack is to fill that price range from the ignored side, since the article itself says "if you like low owned players with upside, this is the range for you."

**6. Part of the field builds dead lineups.** In golf there is no correlation between players, so the dead structure here is a team whose six golfers all need the same single thing to happen — for example carrying both Davis Riley and Rasmus Hojgaard, two golfers $100 apart in salary and 0.5 points apart in projection who the article says are on the board purely as "salary relief." The attack is to make sure no entry pays twice for one bet, because a team holding both cheap $6,000 golfers has really only made one decision, not two.

**7. Skipped — news stampede.** There is no injury, withdrawal or late-news story anywhere in this week's article, so there is no unconfirmed-news mistake to attack this slate.

**8. Skipped — the trap plays losers love.** We only have one logged Round 4 Showdown autopsy in this ledger, so there is no cumulative field-tendency or fish-trap history to point at yet. This section gets stronger after two or three more logged slates.

**The closing point:** because of these six live mistakes, the real number of distinct teams in that 7,134-entry field is far smaller than 7,134 — hundreds of entries are the same handful of builds submitted over and over.

## Build it like a sharp

This is the closing section and the one you act on. You play mostly single entry, which means one team is your entire tournament. Your stated goal is to copy what the sharps do. So this walks through the decisions a sharp makes, in the order they make them, applied to today. The suggestions below describe the sharp archetype's reasoning — you pick the golfers.

### 1. The anchor decision

A sharp anchors this slate from the **$9,000–$10,400 range, not the $11,000 slot**, because the projections are so flat that the extra $1,000 buys almost nothing. Cameron Young at $11,000 projects 40.7 and Patrick Cantlay at $9,800 projects 39.0 — that is $1,200 for 1.7 points. The three realistic anchor candidates are Cameron Young ($11,000, 32.6% owned), Xander Schauffele ($10,400, 26.0%) and Si Woo Kim ($10,000, 17.2%). **Xander and Si Woo are near-identical** — same price shelf, same profile, 9 ownership points apart — so a sharp who is genuinely indifferent between them takes the cheaper, less-owned side, and the article expects both to finish below their projected ownership anyway.

A sharp does **not** take Michael Brennan to zero. Our ledger has that codified: zeroing a top anchor is a voluntary exit, and it cost us the RBC Canadian Open when a top-three projected golfer was taken to 0% and finished the second-highest scorer on the slate. Under-owning Brennan is leverage. Owning none of him is a different thing.

### 2. The leverage piece

A sharp's low-owned golfer this week comes from the **$7,100–$7,700 range**, because that is where six golfers project within 0.6 points of the crowd's favorite while sitting at one third of his ownership. The candidates, with ownership: **Marco Penge** ($7,500, 9.7%), **Johnny Keefer** ($7,700, 7.9%), **Emiliano Grillo** ($7,700, 7.8%), **Stephan Jaeger** ($7,200, 6.5%), **Ricky Castillo** ($7,100, 8.0%) and **Sungjae Im** ($7,300, 9.1%). If a sharp wants the deeper version — a golfer under 5% owned — the names are **Nicolai Hojgaard** ($8,400, 4.0%) and **Jordan Spieth** ($7,400, 4.4%).

Anchor the dose to the observed numbers, not to instinct. The pros in your contests carry a sub-5%-owned golfer in **48.9%** of their lineups; you carry one in **39.7%**. That 9-point gap is the specific thing to close. But the format number matters more than the generic one: our 146-slate Round 4 Showdown study says winners here average about **19% ownership per golfer** with about **1.7 golfers under 10% owned**. Last slate you ran **2.83** low-owned golfers per lineup against the winners' **1.63**, and your two most contrarian teams finished 99.8th and 91.7th percentile. So the target is one or two leverage spots, held firmly — not four.

### 3. What a sharp refuses

A sharp refuses to be the 877th team with the same two golfers. The pairs the field will duplicate most, with counts in the 7,134-entry contest: **Brennan + Young (~877 teams)**, **Brennan + Fowler (~728)**, **Brennan + Xander (~699)**, **Brennan + Kirk (~685)**, **Brennan + Gerard (~678)** and **Young + Fowler (~635)**. Carrying both halves of the top pair means you are sharing that decision with roughly 877 opponents before a single shot is hit. In the 245-entry single-entry contest, that same pair is shared with about 30 of your 244 opponents.

A sharp also refuses to pay twice for one bet. Chris Kirk and Kris Ventura are the article's stated "max 1" — carrying both spends 47.4 ownership points on two golfers in the same tier. Davis Riley and Rasmus Hojgaard are the same idea $100 apart. So are Hideki and Koivun. Each of those pairs is one decision, not two.

### 4. The salary shape

Here is the arithmetic in plain numbers. You have $50,000 for six golfers, which is $8,333 each on average.

| If you anchor with | It costs | Left for the rest | Average per remaining slot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cameron Young ($11,000) | $11,000 | $39,000 for 5 | $7,800 |
| Xander Schauffele ($10,400) | $10,400 | $39,600 for 5 | $7,920 |
| Young + Brennan | $19,600 | $30,400 for 4 | $7,600 |
| Young + Xander + Cantlay | $31,200 | $18,800 for 3 | $6,267 |

That last row is the trap the article describes. Stacking three expensive golfers forces your last three slots into the $6,000 bin, which is exactly why Davis Riley and Rasmus Hojgaard are about to be 20% owned. A sharp picks one or two top golfers, not three.

And the cap forces the real decision. **The six most-owned golfers cost $55,800, and you only have $50,000.** Every team in both contests has to drop at least one of Brennan, Young, Fowler, Xander, Kirk or Gerard. Which one you drop is the actual choice this week — more than which leverage golfer you add.

### 5. Two contests, two different entries

You have two contests with very different shapes. The **$2.5K Albatross** is a 245-entry single entry with balanced payouts — a smaller field where a tight, high-conviction team competes well without going far off the board. The **$3K mini-MAX** is a 7,134-entry contest with top-heavy payouts and you have 34 entries — there, only winning matters, so the contrarian dial goes higher and the anchor-equivalence twins become a sized position across many entries rather than one lineup's pivot. Each entry needs its own one-sentence reason it wins. Two entries answering the same question is one bet paid for twice, which is the exact mistake our CJ Cup autopsy recorded — four lineups all asking "what if the chalk anchor holds and a mid-tier sleeper hits?"

### 6. The pre-lock check

Answer these against the team you actually built, before you lock it.

1. Does it have at least one golfer owned under 10%? (Winners here average about 1.7 of them.)
2. Does it have **no more than two** golfers owned under 10%? (More than that has been penalized in this format — your own last slate proved it.)
3. Does the average ownership across your six golfers land near **19%**, which is about **115% added up**? (Add the six ownership numbers; if the total is over about 130 you are riding the crowd, if it is under about 90 you are over-contrarian for this format.)
4. Does it avoid holding **both** halves of the top duplicated pair (Brennan + Young), and both halves of any equivalence twin (Xander/Si Woo, Gerard/Henley, Kirk/Wallace, Riley/Rasmus)?
5. Can you say in one sentence how this team wins today?
6. Is there at least one golfer who can realistically post a huge single round — the article says 7-under rounds are on the table here?

**Six golfers averaging roughly 19% ownership (about 115% added up), with one or two of them under 10% owned, at least one able to post a monster single round, all six unique against the crowd's top pairs.** For coaching, look at **moklovin** — the tracked pro who has been in 5 of your logged golf contests and beaten you in 2 of them.
