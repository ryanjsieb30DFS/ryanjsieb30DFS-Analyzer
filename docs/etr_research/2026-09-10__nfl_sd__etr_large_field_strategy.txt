As our opponents continue to evolve and the available information improves,
I believe one of the biggest remaining edges in NFL showdown is properly
constructing lineups to maximize correlation while still mitigating the
likelihood that those lineups are heavily duplicated by the field. To start
establishing optimal roster construction theories, we can use years’ worth of
primetime showdown data as our guiding light. Of course, past production
DOES NOT equal future success, but it is, at minimum, a worthy thought
exercise and can potentially play a major role in our decision-making process
on future showdown slates.

optimal CPT is a necessity for showdown profitability. For this exercise, let’s

Ultimately, the most common winning CPT QB stack is also the most
intuitive one. IF a QB is going to finish as the slate’s top overall scorer, THEN
he’s likely supporting multiple pass catchers. Our baseline for CPT QB
lineups should default to pairing him with at least (2) WR/TE teammates and
adjust on a slate-by-slate basis as needed.
Massaging rules to fit slate context is important, to avoid grouping ourselves
out of quality lineups. QBs who derive at least some of their value on the
ground can be more profitably single stacked or in rare instances played
unstacked entirely. There were (8) QBs last season who appeared in at least 10
games and had a rushing share north of 20.0%. These types have more outs to
slate-winning fantasy production while supporting fewer pass catchers.

We know that historically, it’s taken an average of 22.4 DraftKings points,
before the 1.5x multiplier, for a WR to finish as the CPT in top-1% finishing
lineups. How, then, is it possible for QBs to fall short of those lineups so
frequently? The most obvious example is when a single WR soaks up a vast
majority of the team’s pass-catching production while his QB struggles to
produce fantasy points elsewhere. This will occur most often with high-
volume, low-aDOT receivers whose production is elevated by DraftKings’
scoring system.

Salary constraints are another driver of the unstacked CPT WR success rate.
Historically, the average salary, after the 1.5x multiplier, of a CPT WR in top-
1% lineups is $12,550. We also know that the average salary for a starting QB is
$10,647. With at least (1) QB appearing in 92.5% of ALL top-1% lineups, we can
confidently assume most winning lineups will feature at least one of the two
playable options. However, if the opposing QB matches or exceeds the
fantasy production of the wide receiver’s quarterback, it can quickly become
difficult to find the salary needed to play all three. In these instances, we’ll
find the opposing QB in winning lineups.
In order to maximize our win potential in naked CPT WR lineups, it makes
sense to a) prioritize the opposing team’s QB and b) mute the need to play
the same-team WR/TE in FLEX.

Let’s shift gears from optimally building around our preferred CPT to
optimally building around a preferred outcome. Onslaught lineups, as I’ll
refer to them, include five players from one team with just one player from
the opposing team. A possible way to build for blowouts or just lopsided
fantasy production, onslaughts are generally less utilized than more balanced
roster constructions and, at least in my opinion, have relatively
straightforward paths to climbing leaderboards when we’re right, and we can
start by knocking out the bring-back from the opposing side.
