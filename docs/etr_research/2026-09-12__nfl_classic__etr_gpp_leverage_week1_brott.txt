                    NFL DFS is back! After months of debating depth charts, projecting
                    workloads, and talking ourselves into our favorite players, we finally get a
                    full Sunday slate to put those opinions to the test. I’m sure we’ll all feel
                    great about them right up until the first round of goal-line carries.

                    Welcome to the first NFL GPP Leverage column of the 2026 season. I’m
                    excited to be back for my third year writing this article and talking through
                    one of my favorite parts of tournament DFS: how to build lineups that
                    offer leverage against our opponents.

                    For those who are new to Leverage, the goal of this column is to help us
                    think through the slate. Each week, we’ll look at the slate primarily from a
                    macro perspective before providing some micro examples. The individual
                    players make the macro ideas concrete, but understanding the reasoning
                    should help us find additional ways to apply them in our lineups.

                    I’ve shared this quote from my Leverage-writing predecessor Drew
                    Dinkmeyer in each of my first two seasons writing this column, and I’m
                    going to share it again because it captures exactly what I want readers to
                    take away from each article:



                        “The examples I highlight will be in my portfolio of lineups, but if you
                        want to improve considerably as a GPP player, mastering the concepts
                        is more important than copying the answers.”


                    That becomes especially relevant between Friday afternoon and Sunday
                    kickoff. A player who looks like a great leverage option when this article is
                    published can become one of the slate’s most popular plays by kickoff.
                    When that happens, we should revisit what made the play appealing and
                    whether the new ownership changes our decision. Ideally, the discussion

                    With those shifting slate dynamics in mind, I plan to change the structure

                    see the slate playing out as a whole, using individual players and expected
                    chalk to illustrate those ideas. This should help make the column more
                    useful and relevant throughout the weekend. It should also allow me the
                    capacity to include a data analysis project each week — if you ever have an
                    analysis idea you’d like to see included, don’t hesitate to reach out!

                    I also want to preface that my own perspective comes primarily from
                    playing small-field tournaments on DraftKings, but my goal is for the
                    macro ideas discussed here to be useful across DFS platforms and contest
                    sizes. For those particularly interested in FanDuel, we have revamped our
                    coverage for FanDuel NFL this season, and that will include new written

                    Alright, with all of the intros out of the way, let’s get into the slate.




                    Week 1 gives us plenty to work with from a leverage standpoint because
                    even our strongest expectations come with unanswered questions. We can
                    have good reasons to expect an offense to improve or a defense to remain
                    strong and still see something different on Sunday. The challenge is
                    deciding which alternatives are plausible enough, and rewarding enough,
                    to build around. We don’t need every popular assumption to be wrong. A
                    lineup can benefit quite a bit from getting one meaningful disagreement
                    right, especially when the rest of its pieces fit that outcome.

                    In fact, our fantasy-point projections at ETR have historically been roughly

                    While it’s reassuring that our projections have historically held up just as
                    well in Week 1, we certainly will get some spots wrong. And if we can
                    predict or take shots on which spots those might be, we can gain
                    considerable leverage by having our lineups play into assumptions that
                    disagree with the market. Let’s walk through a selection of games on
                    Sunday’s main slate and briefly consider how we can gain leverage by
                    going against the most common assumptions in the market.



                    Jets at Titans

                    The market assumes both teams will struggle significantly on offense, and I
                    expect limited ownership across both offenses. But what if Cam Ward and
                    the Titans take a big leap on offense this season despite their struggles in
                    the preseason? What if Geno Smith is a significant upgrade over the
                    lackluster QB play they had last season, and he can support the plethora of

                    Bears at Panthers

                    The market assumes Chicago’s offense will take a significant leap in its
                    second season under Ben Johnson, with a poor defense creating even
                    more opportunities for high-scoring games. But what if that offensive leap
                    doesn’t happen, or at least takes longer than expected? Down the stretch of
                    2025, the Bears often struggled to generate significant offense until the
                    fourth quarter. Their late-game success gives us reasons for optimism, but
                    we still need to see whether they can produce consistently throughout
                    games. If those earlier struggles carry into this season, we could be getting
                    ahead of ourselves by treating Chicago as one of the league’s best offenses
                    before it has demonstrated that consistency.




                    Buccaneers at Bengals

                    The market assumes Cincinnati will continue to be a team we can count on
                    for shootouts: an excellent offense paired with a defense that struggles to
                    get stops. But what if the Bengals’ defensive improvement down the stretch
                    of 2025 was the beginning of something sustainable? That unit played
                    considerably better after its bye, and they added Dexter Lawrence in the
                    offseason. Joe Burrow has even discussed the possibility that a better
                    overall team won’t need him to throw as much. If the defense has become
                    more reliable, we may need to reconsider both how aggressively we attack
                    Cincinnati with opposing offenses and how much passing volume we
                    expect from the Bengals themselves.

                    Saints at Lions

                    The market assumes Detroit will pick up where it left off offensively, and
                    this is shaping up to be the most popular game on the slate. We have
                    plenty of evidence that the Lions can score, including their success with
                    Dan Campbell calling plays late last season. But what if the transition to
                    Drew Petzing introduces more uncertainty than we’re accounting for?
                    Detroit has a new play-caller, and the offense could regress to the struggles
                    we saw before Campbell assumed play-calling in 2025. The personnel can
                    remain excellent while the timing and execution take a step back. We may
                    be treating the Lions’ offensive efficiency as a constant when some of the
                    conditions that produced it have changed.




                    Bills at Texans

                    The market assumes Houston’s defense will again make life difficult for
                    Buffalo, and there is very little interest in the Bills’ passing game. But what
                    if the addition of D.J. Moore changes how effectively the entire offense
                    functions against even a top defense? Moore gives Buffalo the best receiver
                    they’ve had since Stefon Diggs, potentially changing how defenses allocate
                    coverage against the Bills and creating more space for everyone else. Even
                    if Houston’s defense is excellent, as the market anticipates, Buffalo’s
                    improved passing game could succeed even against the defenses we would
                    normally prefer to avoid.




                    Browns at Jaguars

                    The market assumes Cleveland will once again have one of the offenses we
                    want to avoid, with Jacksonville’s defense attracting plenty of interest. But
                    what if Todd Monken can make the Browns a functional offense sooner
                    than expected? The quarterback concerns are real, but what if Monken can
                    reduce how often this offense needs its quarterback to overcome difficult
                    situations? Easier throws and fewer obvious passing downs could make

                    Falcons at Steelers

                    The market assumes Pittsburgh will remain a fairly limited offense, and
                    Atlanta’s quarterback uncertainty makes it easy to dismiss this game as a
                    whole. But what if the reunion of Aaron Rodgers and Mike McCarthy
                    produces a more efficient Steelers offense than we’re used to seeing?
                    Rodgers has discussed playing more under center and using more play-
                    action and movement to improve early-down efficiency. His familiarity
                    with McCarthy’s system could help those changes translate more quickly
                    than they would for a quarterback learning everything from scratch. If
                    Pittsburgh can consistently create manageable situations and sustain
                    drives, its reputation as an unappealing offense could leave the field slow to
                    recognize a better Steelers offense.




                    Cardinals at Chargers

                    The market assumes Mike McDaniel will unlock an excellent Chargers
                    offense while L.A.’s defense remains a difficult matchup for opponents.
                    Most of the enthusiasm for this game is on the Los Angeles side. But what
                    if the coaching change on defense matters just as much as the one on
                    offense? Jesse Minter has left for Baltimore, and Chris O’Leary is taking
                    over after spending last season coordinating Western Michigan’s defense. If
                    the Chargers take a step back defensively, we may be too quick to dismiss
                    Arizona’s offense based on the matchup, especially after the offensive
                    explosions we saw from the Cardinals last season when trailing. McDaniel’s
                    success could make Chargers games even more appealing if both offenses

                    Dolphins at Raiders

                    The market assumes Miami will be terrible on both offense and defense,
                    making the Dolphins an easy team to attack and a difficult one to trust. But
                    what if the offense is more functional than the overall assessment suggests?
                    Bobby Slowik and the Dolphins have an opportunity to build around
                    Malik Willis’ mobility, forcing defenses to account for the quarterback as a
                    runner and potentially creating more room for the running backs and
                    easier throws off play-action. Miami’s defense could be every bit as bad as
                    expected while the offense proves capable of sustaining drives and
                    contributing to high-scoring games. We should consider whether the
                    expectation of a bad team is making us too dismissive of what this
                    particular offensive system might do well.




                    Commanders at Eagles

                    The market assumes Washington will struggle offensively in Philadelphia,
                    especially after the loss of OT Laremy Tunsil, and the lack of interest
                    suggests the field is comfortable waiting to see improvement before buying
                    back in. But what if the Commanders’ injury-disrupted 2025 season is
                    giving us the wrong starting point? A healthy Jayden Daniels gives the
                    offense a chance to regain the efficiency and explosiveness it showed in
                    2024 — and remember: Daniels and Terry McLaurin arguably didn’t play
                    a single game together in 2025 where both were at full strength.
                    Philadelphia is a difficult opening test, but we should consider whether that
                    matchup is making us too quick to overlook a potentially stronger version
                    of the Commanders.

                    Finally, I want to consider how efficient market ownership has historically
                    been in the opening weeks of NFL seasons. To be completely transparent, I
                    started down the path of comparing historical ownership efficiencies
                    between Week 1 and other weeks with the plan to demonstrate my
                    hypothesis that market ownerships have signaled extreme overconfidence
                    in how NFL teams will operate at the start of a new season. But that wasn’t
                    the full story, and the field deserves some credit here.

                    For the analysis shown in the tables below, I matched pairs of players at the
                    same position whose salaries were within $500 and whose final ETR
                    projections were within one fantasy point. One player had to carry at least
                    twice the actual ownership of the other, with a minimum gap of five
                    percentage points to qualify. I limited the sample to regular-season Weeks
                    1–17 and slates with at least eight games, then compared each group’s
                    average ownership and frequency of scoring at least 25 DraftKings points.
                    This gives us a way to examine how much additional upside the field’s
                    preferred players delivered relative to similarly priced and projected
                    alternatives, and whether that trade-off differed between Week 1 and the
                    rest of the season. Of course, reaching 25 DK points is just one measure of
                    upside, and it doesn’t capture everything that determines a player’s
                    tournament value.

                    Even when choosing between players with similar salaries and ETR
                    projections, the more popular player generally performed better. In Week
                    1, the higher-owned player outscored his counterpart approximately 60%
                    of the time in the Milly Maker and 54% in The Spy. The more popular
                    players also reached 25 DK points more frequently. There is useful
                    information in ownership, and we should consider why the field prefers
                    one player before automatically gravitating toward the other.

                    But the size of that preference matters. In Week 1 Spy contests, the lower-
                    owned players carried about one-quarter of the ownership while reaching
                    25 DK points roughly 60% as often. That is an interesting trade-off when
                    we’re choosing between otherwise comparable options. The Milly Maker
                    results were less appealing, as the lower-owned players carried about one-
                    third of the ownership and reached 25 points about one-third as often.
                    Later in the season, the ceiling gap narrowed considerably in both contests
                    while the ownership gap remained substantial.

                    The ownership premium was generally better supported by these results in
                    Week 1 than later in the season, which gives us a reason to respect the
                    field’s preferences even with all of the unanswered questions. But there is
                    still room to consider whether a popular assumption is carrying too much
                    weight in our opponents’ lineups. For the games discussed above, we can

                    use our projections to help identify alternatives that remain competitive
                    without requiring everything to break their way. When those alternatives
                    also offer a substantial ownership discount, we don’t need to believe they
                    are the most likely outcome to have interest in building around them.

                    As is always the case with NFL data, I want to caution that we’re dealing
                    with very small sample sizes here, particularly when it comes to the
                    number of years of Week 1 data. It is certainly possible that over time we
                    would see the Week 1 rates regress toward those of Weeks 2-17.




                    Putting it all together, I don’t see a compelling reason to stray further from
                    projections or chase lower ownership simply because it’s Week 1 — even
                    though I came into this analysis expecting to find one. Yet we should still
                    recognize that at least a few teams will shock us with their performances
                    every season. And if we can find those surprises at low ownership in Week
                    1, we can gain significant positive leverage against our opponents.
